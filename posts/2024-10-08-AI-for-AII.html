Do we all need AI in the 21st century?

For those coming of age already already in the 21st century, it may seem that today’s insane pace in news, style trends, and technology is how it’s always been, but for the previous generations there was a reasonably precise moment when things changed radically from a manageable amount of information to a deluge of data. Yes, The Internet. When computers became widespread and replaced stacks of paper for most tasks, when we no longer had to walk to a store to buy a music record (which we woud then listen to for months before getting a different record) and – shortly after – when phones stopped being attached to the wall with the single function of calling someone who needed to be the in exact place where theirs was attached as well.

Just after data started being generated at this crazy rate, it started being recorded, creating an exponentially-growing need for storage. There was no centralized index either, and it would be humanly impossible to look up stuff there anyway, so Yahoo, Altavista, and later Google dominated internet search. It took another decade or so for us to start hearing about Data Mining, Big Data, or Machine Learning (ML), and just over couple of decades until the early 20s when powerful chatbots (i.e. LLMs) were released into the wild. That’s when some of us (who were old enough not to grow up with Netflix, and got to watch Terminator a dozen times or more) started considering how worried we should really be about Skyet becoming self aware (for those who didn’t, it meant it’s all fun and games until the chatbots decide earth is better of without us humans, and become killer-bots).

Doomsday predictions and jokes aside, it seems like in the past year either everything became AI-powered and despite the dubious AI “applications”, companies like Goldman-Sachs and Bloomberg are certain there is room for growth and forecast a market in the trillions, so most people don’t want to miss out on being at the forefront of this exciting technology – especially if your competirors are already trying to gain an edge through AI (or at least claim to be). But can the common folk get a general grasp on AI when even key players can’t really agree on what AI is? My suggestion: start from the basics. I know it’s difficult, things seem to move too fast to take a step back and examine the foundation of anything, but I believe a general operational definition for the new millenium is possible – I promise I’ll do my best to provide a high density of information (and sarcasm) that is compatible with our current century.

The term Artificial Intelligence was originally coined mid-20th century by John McCarthy along with collaborators like Claude Shannon, and meant then what most people think it means now: intelligence like that of humans that allows computers or machines to do things that we see as exclusively human: think, reflect, decide, take responsibility, be creative. Nevertheless, 21st century AI does nothing of that sort, which prompted capitains of industry to rebrand it as “Narrow AI” and reserve the term “Artificial General Intelligence” to what was simply AI before – with that, broad claims are still made about narrow achievements, but ascribing intelligence to simple algorithms has the side effect of making AI an overly generic term that borders on the meaningless. A more appropriate term would probably be Machine Learning, and even that has been hyped up by claiming classical statistical methods developed over two hundred years ago like linear regression or making up terms like “Statistical Learning”. Long story (somewhat) short(er): as much as it may sound cool and futuristic to have an AI-powered fridge or thermal paste, most of what is advertised is actual bullshit, and even useful (and cool) applications like chatGPT do not rise to the level of intelligence that Shannon dreamed up – worse, suggesting the bleeding edge does things similar to a human is bullshit as well. There is* a* lot* of* debate* and* online* controversy* around whether Artificial Intelligence is just glorified Statistics. The \(AI = GS\) crowd can truthfully clain that, although the practice in the wild is different, the mathematics of Machine Learning is exactly the same as that of Statistical Modeling (SM, AI & ML’s less popular cousin), but the questions remain: Should I be doing something in the AI front; is AI/ML useless after all?

The different camps may hold strong views on their positions; there is no veredict on them – and it probably doesn’t matter. What is not controversial is that applications that were impossible as recently as a decade ago are now possible. That is due to the amount of computing power not available until the turn of the century, and especially the amount of data, without which all computing power would be useless (hence the early AI alias, Big Data). We have seen what chatbots and text-based image generators can do, and they are already being used in all sorts of applications. Nevertheless, in the last couple of years it became clear that shoving AI into whatever problem or slapping an “AI-powered” label onto a product, which may or may not use ML at all, does not automatically solve any research or business problem (except that of companies selling AI solutions that may or may not contain any ML at all), in addition to being pointlessly expensive.

Instead, Human Intelligence (HI!) is still required to solve any and all problems – AI/ML is set of exciting tools, but to leverage their potential it is essential to answer the following questions:

  1. What are the real-world mathematical and statistical bases of Machine Learning: what can it actually do, what is hype, and what it Sci-Fi?
  2. What are the challenges your organization is facing, and what would data-driven solutions look like?
  3. What is your organization’s Data Strategy

Only after having some answers for those quetions can AI/ML/SM be used effectively.
